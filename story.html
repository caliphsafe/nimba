D’MBA

The story of D’mba.

Before the emblem, there was a dance. The Baga communities of coastal Guinea gave D’mba a body, a place in the agricultural year, and a role in the lives of the people who gathered around her.

A living inheritance

Rooted in a place.
Carried through time.

D’mba’s story moves between the work of the carver, the gathering of a community, and the wider image of Guinea. Each holds a different part of her meaning.

  1. The coastal roots

    Baga communities, Guinea

    The coastal communities who gave D’mba her meaning lived with the rhythms of rice cultivation. Planting, harvest, family life, and shared gatherings formed the world around the masquerade.

  2. The carved form

    An image of care

    The arched profile, patterned coiffure, and mature body give form to an ideal of motherhood. The figure honors a woman who has nurtured many children, and the strength that care requires.

  3. The shared performance

    Wood becomes movement

    Supported on a dancer’s shoulders and accompanied by cloth and raffia, D’mba appeared at occasions including planting and harvest celebrations, weddings, and ceremonies of remembrance. Practices varied by community.

  4. 1985

    Guinea’s gift to the world

    The Republic of Guinea presented a wooden Nimba to the United Nations. A form rooted in Baga life took its place on an international stage as a gift from the country.

  5. Today / Nimba

    A connection carried forward

    Our collection brings the emblem into daily life. The clothing is contemporary; the attachment reaches back to the people, craftsmanship, and memories behind the form.

From the highlands to the coast

Baga oral histories trace ancestral roots to Futa Jallon, the highlands in Guinea’s interior. Accounts of the journey vary by community. Many elders locate the making of D’mba on the coast, after their ancestors had settled there.

Rice cultivation shaped life on the Atlantic coast. The agricultural calendar, with its seasons of planting and harvest, gave the masquerade a rhythm. The headdress also appeared at weddings, funerals, and gatherings held to honor visitors.

The woman in the wood

A Baga carver could shape a monumental D’mba from a single piece of wood. Look closely at the curve of the nose, the braided crest, and the incised lines around the face and along the chest. The coiffure recalls Fulbe hairstyles and Baga memories of Futa Jallon.

The long breasts honor a mature woman who has nursed and raised many children. D’mba embodies a model of care and strength. Fertility belongs to that meaning, both in the life of the family and in the hoped-for abundance of the fields.

“Care is a strength a community can recognize.”

When D’mba danced

Four carved supports steadied the headdress on a dancer’s shoulders. Cloth and a raffia skirt hid the performer, who looked out through openings between the breasts. In documented performances, drummers led the way into the village gathering place.

People took part in the performance. Women led songs and men joined the chorus. In some accounts, people reached toward the headdress or tossed rice as an expression of hope for fertility and a generous harvest. Practices varied from one community to another.

A tradition carried forward

Written records document D’mba by the late nineteenth century. Political and religious pressure interrupted public performances in the mid-twentieth century. Museum accounts describe renewed performances in the late twentieth century, as communities brought the form back into public life.

In 1985, Guinea gave a wooden Nimba to the United Nations. The figure also appeared on older 5,000-franc notes. In 2023, Guinea adopted the profile as its country brand. Each public use widened its audience, while the name D’mba still points back to the Baga artists and communities who made the figure meaningful.

The story we wear

A connection worth carrying.

Nimba begins with an acknowledgment: this form comes from Baga artists and communities. Its associations with care, maturity, and abundance reach beyond a simple label of “fertility goddess.” The shoulder mask belongs to a history of performance, craftsmanship, and communal life.

Our clothing is a contemporary expression of attachment to Guinea. It offers a way to carry that connection into everyday life, while keeping the people behind the emblem in view.
